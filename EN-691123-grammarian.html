The Grammarian: Five Small Fixes That Make a Big Difference
The first issue of our weekly newsletter on better writing. This week: affect or effect, fewer or less, a Thinglish trap, a grammar myth and a word worth knowing.

Welcome to The Grammarian, The Siam Times’ weekly note on writing better English. Each issue offers a few quick lessons you can use the same day: a confusing pair, a common mistake, a myth to retire and a word to add to your collection. It is short enough to read over coffee, and useful enough to keep.

Confusing pair: affect or effect?

Affect is usually a verb meaning “to influence.” Effect is usually a noun meaning “a result.”

The haze affected flights at Chiang Mai airport.

The haze had a serious effect on tourism.

A memory trick: Affect is an Action; Effect is an End result.

There are exceptions. Effect can be a verb meaning “to bring about,” as in “to effect change,” and psychologists use affect as a noun for visible emotion. You will rarely need either.

Common mistake: fewer or less?

Use fewer for things you can count and less for things you cannot.

Fewer cars, less traffic.

Fewer plastic bags, less plastic.

Time, money and distance are the exceptions. Although they are numbers, we usually treat them as amounts: “less than 30 minutes,” “less than 500 baht,” “less than 10 kilometres.”

Thinglish fix: “I’m boring”

Thai distinguishes เบื่อ, to feel bored, from น่าเบื่อ, boring. English makes the same distinction with word endings, and it is easy to pick the wrong one. Adjectives ending in -ed describe how someone feels; those ending in -ing describe what causes the feeling.

I’m bored. (I feel bored.)

I’m boring. (I make other people feel bored.)

The same goes for interested and interesting, tired and tiring, excited and exciting, and confused and confusing. A dull lecture is boring. The students who sit through it are bored.

Myth of the week: never end a sentence with a preposition

Many of us were taught that a sentence must not end with a preposition such as with, about or to. The “rule” is usually traced to the 17th-century poet John Dryden, who wanted English to behave more like Latin. It was never part of natural English.

“Who are you talking to?” is perfectly good English. “To whom are you talking?” is correct, too, but sounds stiff in most situations. A famous retort, often attributed to Winston Churchill, mocks the rule: “This is the sort of English up with which I will not put.” There is no good evidence that Churchill said it, but the joke still lands.

Word of the week: concise

Concise (adjective): giving a lot of information clearly, in few words.

Her report was concise: two pages, three findings and one clear recommendation.

Concise is not the same as short. A short answer may leave things out; a concise one leaves out only what is unnecessary. Related forms: concisely (adverb), conciseness (noun).

This week’s challenge

Find one sentence in your recent writing and make it shorter without losing any meaning. For example:

Before: At this point in time, we are currently in the process of reviewing the applications. (15 words)

After: We are reviewing the applications. (5 words)

Nothing was lost, and two-thirds of the words were cut.
