A Pact Aims to Clear Southeast Asia’s Skies, but a Border Blind Spot Remains
A trilateral initiative between Thailand, Laos, and Myanmar seeks to curb toxic smog. Yet in Thailand’s lower northeast, unregulated burning in Cambodia keeps the air dangerously thick, prompting calls for stricter domestic enforcement.
Smoke rises from a paddy field as agricultural stubble is set ablaze to clear the land. THAI POST

The annual scourge of transboundary haze has long cast a toxic pall over Southeast Asia, posing a dire threat to public health and regional economies. Driven largely by the rampant burning of forests and agricultural lands, the choking smog has prompted a renewed push for a diplomatic solution. Enter the CLEAR Sky Strategy, an ambitious trilateral pact forged by Thailand, Laos, and Myanmar, alongside a joint action plan spanning 2024 to 2030. The initiative’s goal is as clear as its name: to systematically reduce regional fire hotspots and bring PM2.5 levels down to World Health Organization standards.

The collaborative effort targets the northern reaches of Southeast Asia, a region reliably blanketed in smoke during the early months of the year. Anchored by the “CLEAR” framework, the strategy emphasizes continuous hotspot reduction, cross-border cooperation at every level of government, and the rapid sharing of air quality data. To bring the plan to the ground, officials are deploying advanced technologies, including real-time forecasting platforms and wildfire risk mapping, backed by Thai state agencies and the National Research Council.

Yet, for all its promise, the framework offers little relief to Thailand’s lower northeastern provinces. In Nakhon Ratchasima, Chaiyaphum, Buri Ram, and Surin, the skies remain stubbornly gray. The primary culprit is agricultural burning across the border in Cambodia — a country conspicuously absent from the current CLEAR Sky arrangement. Deep-seated military and national security sensitivities along the frontier have stalled environmental diplomacy, leaving efforts to negotiate a burn ban with Phnom Penh largely deadlocked.

With agricultural fires in neighboring Cambodia proving politically intractable, experts argue that the most viable immediate solution lies within Thailand's own borders. If diplomatic avenues remain closed, the Thai government must pivot inward, enforcing existing bans on open-air burning in forests and farmlands with uncompromising rigor. By pairing strict legal enforcement with sustainable alternatives for managing crop residue, the nation must build a domestic shield against the silent, seasonal threat of toxic air.
