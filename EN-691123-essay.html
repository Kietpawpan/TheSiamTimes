How to Write a Compelling Essay, From First Idea to Final Draft
A good essay is an argument with a shape. Find something worth saying, say it early and support it with evidence the reader cannot ignore.

The word “essay” comes from the French essai, meaning “an attempt.” The French writer Michel de Montaigne gave the name to his collection of short reflections, first published in 1580, in which he tried out ideas on subjects ranging from friendship to cannibals. More than four centuries later, the best essays keep that spirit: a mind working through a question in front of the reader.

Whether you are writing for a university application, an exam or the opinion page, the craft is the same.

Start with a question, not a topic

A topic is a subject, such as plastic waste. A question is a problem worth thinking about: why do shoppers who carry a cloth bag to the supermarket still accept plastic ones at the fresh market? Questions lead to arguments. Topics lead to lists of facts.

Brainstorm freely, then choose the question that interests you most. Your interest will show on the page, and so will your boredom.

Write a thesis you can defend

The thesis is your answer to the question, in one or two sentences. It is the most important sentence in the essay. A strong thesis is specific, arguable and focused.

Weak: Plastic is bad for the environment.

Strong: When Thailand’s major retailers stopped handing out free plastic bags in 2020, shoppers adapted quickly, which suggests the same approach could work in fresh markets.

The weak thesis states something nobody disputes. The strong one makes a claim that someone could argue with, and it tells the reader exactly what the essay will set out to show.

Hook the reader

Your first sentence must earn the second. Open with something concrete: a surprising fact, a short scene, a sharp question or a problem. Avoid openings that could start any essay on any subject, such as “Since the dawn of time…,” “In today’s modern world…” or a dictionary definition.

Weak: In today’s world, the environment is very important.

Better: Why do we carry a cloth bag to the supermarket, then accept five plastic ones at the morning market?

Then move from the hook to your thesis within the first paragraph or two.

Build the body, one paragraph at a time

Each body paragraph should make one point in support of your thesis. A reliable pattern has four parts.

Point: a topic sentence stating the paragraph’s claim.

Evidence: facts, figures, examples or quotations that support it.

Explanation: how the evidence proves the point. This is where many essays fall short. Do not assume the evidence speaks for itself; show the reader why it matters.

Link: a sentence that ties the point back to the thesis or leads into the next paragraph.

Here is a quick test of structure. Read only the first sentence of each paragraph. Together, they should summarise your argument. If they do not, rearrange or rewrite until they do.

Face the other side

A compelling essay does not pretend there are no objections. Present the strongest counterargument fairly, then answer it. In our example, a critic might argue that new rules for fresh markets would burden small vendors who work on thin margins. That is a real concern, and the essay is stronger for addressing it, perhaps by proposing a gradual phase-in or help with the cost of alternatives. Taking on the best opposing case shows confidence and earns the reader’s trust.

End with more than a summary

A conclusion that merely repeats the introduction wastes the reader’s time. Restate your thesis briefly in fresh words, then go one step further. Explain the wider significance, suggest what should happen next or return to the image from your opening with new meaning. Leave the reader with something to think about.

Revise without mercy

First drafts are for finding out what you think. Second drafts are for saying it clearly. If you can, put the draft away for a day, then reread it as a stranger would.

Fix the big things first. Is the thesis clear? Does every paragraph support it? Is anything missing or repeated? Only then polish the sentences and words. Cut anything that does not serve the argument, even a line you love.

Finally, read the essay aloud. Your ear will catch clumsy sentences that your eye passed over.

A word on exams

Under time pressure, spend the first five minutes planning. Note your thesis and three supporting points before you write a word of the essay. A well-planned essay in simple language almost always scores better than an unplanned one full of impressive vocabulary. Save a few minutes at the end to check your grammar and spelling.
