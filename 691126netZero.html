Thailand Moved Its Net-Zero Goal Forward 15 Years. Now Comes the Hard Part.
A pledge to reach net-zero emissions by 2050 has put Thailand on paper among the more ambitious emerging economies. Its unfinished climate law and power plan will decide whether the promise holds.

A year ago this month, Thailand quietly rewrote its climate ambitions. In a new national climate plan submitted to the United Nations on November 4, 2025, the government pledged to cut net greenhouse gas emissions to 152 million tonnes of carbon dioxide equivalent by 2035, a 47 percent reduction from 2019. More striking still, it committed to reaching net-zero emissions by 2050. The previous target, set in 2022, had been 2065.

The Ministry of Natural Resources and Environment described the change as accelerating Thailand’s net-zero target by 15 years to align with the 1.5°C pathway. On paper, it puts the kingdom among the more ambitious emerging economies in Asia. The harder question, as the plan’s first anniversary passes, is whether the laws, power stations and budgets needed to deliver it are falling into place.

Start with where the emissions come from. According to Thailand’s latest inventory, submitted to the UN in December 2024, the country emitted 385.9 million tonnes in 2022, not counting forests and land use. Energy, which includes electricity, transport and fuel burned in factories, accounted for almost two-thirds. Agriculture, much of it methane from flooded rice paddies and livestock, contributed nearly 18 percent. Industrial processes such as cement-making added about 10 percent, and waste most of the rest.

Forests do a great deal of the work in Thailand’s accounts. Once the carbon they absorb is subtracted, net emissions in 2022 fell to 278 million tonnes, meaning more than a quarter of gross emissions are offset on paper by land and trees. Because the 2035 target is set in net terms, keeping those forests standing and growing is as important to the plan as building solar farms.

The law meant to drive the transition is not yet in force. The cabinet approved the principles of a Climate Change Act in December 2025, days before the House of Representatives was dissolved for a general election. The 205-section bill, which would make emissions reporting mandatory, create an emissions trading system, introduce a carbon tax and a border measure and establish a climate fund, is still being reviewed by the Council of State. The Department of Climate Change and Environment expects it to take effect in 2027.

The power plan is the other missing piece. A draft Power Development Plan unveiled in 2024, which promised that renewables would supply 51 percent of electricity by 2037, was never adopted. Its successor, a draft plan covering 2026 to 2050, would add about 50.9 gigawatts of capacity by 2037, including 24.3 gigawatts of solar, 14.5 gigawatts of battery storage and 2.7 gigawatts of wind. But it also includes 9.1 gigawatts of new gas-fired plants, and every scenario in the plan builds more gas. The draft went to public hearing in September and has not yet been approved.

That matters because power plants last for decades. A gas plant approved today could still be running in 2060, a decade after the net-zero deadline, unless it is retired early at a cost, or fitted with technology to capture its carbon that remains expensive and unproven at scale. The cleaner the plan that is finally adopted, the less Thailand will have to undo later.

Money is another constraint. The government has prepared an investment plan to attract 230 billion baht in international climate finance, and the new climate plan states plainly that meeting its targets depends on robust international support. The failure of the COP31 climate summit in Antalya last weekend to agree its main decision will not make that support easier to find.

There are reasons for optimism. Solar power has become one of the cheapest ways to generate electricity, and data centre investors, carmakers and exporters facing Europe’s carbon border levy are all demanding clean power. Projects such as the floating solar panels on the Sirindhorn Dam reservoir in Ubon Ratchathani show how existing infrastructure can be put to cleaner use.

Thailand has set a destination. Over the next year, the passage of the climate law and the final shape of the power plan will show whether it has also chosen a route.
