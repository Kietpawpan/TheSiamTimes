Where Thai and English Part Ways: The Grammar Behind Our Most Common Mistakes
Most errors Thai writers make in English are not random. They follow the rules of Thai, and once you see the pattern, you can fix it.

Ask an English teacher in Bangkok which sentence they correct most often, and many will give the same answer: “In Bangkok has many temples.” Every word in it is English. The grammar is Thai.

In Thai, ในกรุงเทพฯ มีวัดมากมาย is a perfectly built sentence. The verb มี means both “have” and “there is,” and it needs no subject. English insists on one. The fix is either “There are many temples in Bangkok” or “Bangkok has many temples.”

That example holds the key to grammar for second-language writers. Mistakes are rarely careless. They are usually the first language showing through, and they cluster in a few predictable places. Grammar governs how words change form; syntax governs the order in which they are arranged. Thai and English differ on both.

Every sentence needs a subject

Thai often drops the subject when context makes it clear. “ไปแล้ว,” literally “went already,” is a complete thought. In English, almost every clause needs a subject, even an empty one. That is why English has the “dummy” subjects it and there: “It is raining,” “It is three o’clock,” “There is a problem.” None of these refers to anything. They exist because the sentence needs its subject slot filled.

A rule of thumb: if a sentence begins with a verb or a place phrase and has no clear doer, check it. “Is very hot today” becomes “It is very hot today.”

Time lives in the verb

Thai marks time with words around the verb, such as เคย, กำลัง, จะ and แล้ว, while the verb itself never changes. English changes the verb. “Yesterday I go to the market” sounds natural to a Thai ear because “yesterday” already says when. In English, the verb must agree: “Yesterday I went to the market.”

The perfect tenses cause the most trouble because Thai has no exact match. A useful shortcut: use the present perfect (have plus past participle) when the time is unfinished or unstated, and the simple past when you name a finished time.

I have lived in Khorat for ten years. (I still live there.)

I lived in Khorat for ten years. (I no longer do.)

I have visited Chiang Mai. (At some point in my life.)

I visited Chiang Mai last year. (A finished time, so no have.)

“I have visited Chiang Mai last year” is a common error. Once you name a finished time, the present perfect no longer fits.

Count the nouns

Thai nouns do not change for number. Classifiers do the counting: แมวสามตัว, “cat three body.” English marks the plural on the noun itself, even when a number already makes the meaning plain: “three cats,” not “three cat.” It also adds an -s to a present-tense verb when the subject is a single person or thing: “She works,” “The office opens at eight.” That small -s is one of the most frequently dropped letters in learner English. Read your verbs aloud and listen for it.

English also divides nouns into those you can count and those you cannot. You can make three suggestions but not three advices; you can own many chairs but only much furniture. Words that are uncountable in English, among them advice, information, equipment, luggage, news, research and evidence, take a singular verb and no -s: “The news is good,” “This information is useful.” To count them, add a unit: “two pieces of advice.”

The little words: a, an and the

Thai has no articles at all, which makes a, an and the the hardest words in English for many Thai writers. A simplified rule covers most cases. Use a or an the first time you mention a single countable thing the reader does not yet know about. Use the once you both know which one you mean.

I bought a book. The book was about rice farming.

Use the when there is only one of something (the sun, the Chao Phraya River) and with superlatives (the best, the largest). Use no article for general statements about plural or uncountable nouns: “Dogs are loyal,” “Water is precious.”

Choose between a and an by sound, not spelling: a university, an hour, an MBA.

Adjectives need a verb

In Thai, an adjective such as สวย can serve as the whole predicate: เธอสวย, “she beautiful.” English needs a linking verb: “She is beautiful.” The same habit produces “The food very delicious.” Add the missing is.

Word order trips writers up, too. Thai puts the intensifier after the verb, as in ชอบมาก, “like much,” which leads to “I very like it.” In English, very modifies adjectives and adverbs, not verbs. Say “I like it very much” or “I really like it.”

Questions and short answers

Thai forms a yes-no question by adding a particle such as ไหม or หรือเปล่า to the end of a statement. English moves the verb: “You are hungry” becomes “Are you hungry?” If there is no helping verb, English borrows do: “Do you like durian?” Short answers echo the helping verb: “Yes, I do,” “No, I can’t.” Answering “Can you swim?” with “Can” or “Yes, can” is Thai grammar in English clothes.

Negative questions are a trap. Asked “Don’t you like durian?” a Thai speaker who dislikes it may answer “Yes,” meaning “Yes, that’s right, I don’t.” In English, answer the fact, not the question: “No, I don’t.”

How to practise

Grammar improves fastest when you study your own errors rather than a textbook’s. Keep a list of the corrections you receive; most writers find that a handful of patterns account for most of their mistakes. Then check one pattern at a time. Read a draft once for verb tenses, once for plurals and once for articles.

AI tools can help. Ask one to correct a paragraph and, more importantly, to explain each change. But do not accept every change blindly. The goal is to learn the rule so well that you no longer need the correction.

Grammar is not about passing tests. It is how a reader knows who did what, and when. Get those right, and your ideas have a clear road to travel.
