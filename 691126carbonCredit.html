From Bangkok’s Buses to Community Forests, Thailand Learns to Sell Carbon
Thailand made history with the world’s first transfer of carbon credits under the Paris Agreement. Its domestic market is growing fast, but questions about price, quality and who benefits remain.

Every electric bus that replaces a diesel one on Bangkok’s roads cuts a little pollution from the city’s air. Since late 2023, those cuts have also become an export. Under Article 6.2 of the Paris Agreement, which lets countries trade emission reductions, Thailand and Switzerland completed the world’s first transfer of such credits, known as internationally transferred mitigation outcomes, or ITMOs: 1,916 tonnes of carbon dioxide avoided by the Bangkok E-Bus Programme in 2022.

The programme, run by Energy Absolute and financed in part by Switzerland’s KliK Foundation, has kept selling. In September, a further transfer of 57,327 ITMOs from its 2025 operations brought the programme’s cumulative total to 108,960. Switzerland counts those tonnes towards its own climate target; Thailand, in turn, can no longer count them towards its own.

That trade-off sits at the heart of the international market. Every credit Thailand sells abroad is a reduction it must make up elsewhere to meet its own goal of cutting net emissions by 47 percent by 2035. Thailand has since signed further deals. Japan issued the first Thai credits under its Joint Crediting Mechanism in November 2025, for 1,009 tonnes from a five-megawatt floating solar plant in Prachinburi, and Singapore and Thailand signed an implementation agreement in August 2025, followed by a call for projects this March.

At home, the market runs through the Thailand Voluntary Emission Reduction Program, known as T-VER, operated by the Thailand Greenhouse Gas Management Organization. According to the organisation’s data, 683 projects were registered between fiscal years 2014 and 2026, expected to cut about 17.2 million tonnes a year. A stricter tier, Premium T-VER, designed for credits that meet international standards, had 11 projects, all in forestry and agriculture.

Prices tell a revealing story. Since 2016, about 4.9 million tonnes of T-VER credits have changed hands for roughly 423 million baht, an average of about 86 baht a tonne. Credits from renewable energy projects, which make up most of the supply, have sold for around 40 baht. Credits from forestry and farming have averaged about 411 baht, reflecting buyers’ preference for nature-based projects, especially those that draw carbon out of the air. In February, the cabinet cleared the way for carbon credits to be traded as futures on the Thailand Futures Exchange.

Farming may be the next frontier. A programme backed by Germany’s development agency GIZ and the KliK Foundation, running from 2026 to 2030, aims to train 30,000 rice farmers in four provinces in alternate wetting and drying, a technique that drains paddies periodically to cut methane, with the reductions intended for sale under Article 6.2.

Forest credits, however, are where the hardest questions lie. A 2024 report by the Heinrich Böll Foundation counted 87 forestry projects in the T-VER system covering about 121 community forests, and warned of risks to the land rights of Karen and other communities living in and around them. Who owns the carbon in a forest that villagers have protected for generations, and who decides how the money is spent, are questions Thai law has not fully answered.

The government has signalled some movement. In May, the Ministry of Natural Resources and Environment said the Royal Forest Department would give up its 10 percent share of carbon credits in favour of local communities. It is a step towards a basic principle of fair benefit-sharing: those who protect a forest should share in the value it creates.

Carbon credits can channel real money to clean buses, rooftop solar and villages that guard their forests. But a credit is only as good as the reduction behind it, and only as fair as the bargain struck with the people who make it possible. As Thailand prepares to launch a mandatory emissions trading system under its pending Climate Change Act, getting those foundations right will matter far more than the volume of credits sold.
