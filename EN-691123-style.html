Sounding Right: How Style and Tone Shape What Your Reader Hears
Thai speakers soften a request with ครับ or ค่ะ. English has no such words, so writers must build politeness into the sentence itself.

In Thai, the same request can be made polite simply by adding a particle. ส่งไฟล์ให้หน่อยครับ is courteous; drop the ครับ and the tone hardens. English has no equivalent. Translate the polite Thai literally, as “Send me the file, please,” and an English reader may hear an order, even with the please.

That is the heart of tone: not what you say, but how it sounds to the person reading it. Style is the set of choices that produce the tone, including which words you pick, how long your sentences run and whether you write in the active or the passive voice. Grammar decides whether a sentence is correct. Style decides whether it works.

Politeness lives in the structure

English softens requests through grammar. Questions are softer than commands, past forms are softer than present ones, and conditional forms are softest of all.

Send me the report. (Direct; can sound abrupt.)

Can you send me the report? (Neutral.)

Could you send me the report? (Polite.)

Would you mind sending me the report when you have a moment? (Very polite.)

Words such as just, perhaps and possibly soften further: “I just wanted to check whether the report is ready.” Use them in moderation. Too many, and the writer sounds unsure.

The opposite problem is just as common. Writers who try hard to be polite can sound stiff or old-fashioned. “Kindly be informed that the meeting is postponed” and “Please do the needful” survive in some offices across Asia, but to most international readers they sound dated. “Please note that the meeting has been postponed” does the same job.

Choose your register

Register is the level of formality. A message to a friend on LINE, a report for a government agency and a speech at a wedding each call for a different register.

Formal English favours complete sentences, no contractions and longer words of Latin origin, such as commence, purchase and inform. Informal English uses contractions, phrasal verbs and short everyday words such as start, buy and tell. Neither is better. The mistake is choosing the wrong one, or mixing them. “We regret to inform you that ur application was unsuccessful” jars because it lurches between two registers in a single sentence.

Most modern professional writing sits in the middle: friendly but careful. Contractions are now common in business email. Slang and emoji usually are not.

Prefer plain words

In his 1946 essay “Politics and the English Language,” George Orwell offered six rules for clear writing. Three are worth memorising:

Never use a long word where a short one will do.

If it is possible to cut a word out, always cut it out.

Never use the passive where you can use the active.

His last rule is the wisest: “Break any of these rules sooner than say anything outright barbarous.” They are guides, not laws.

Plain English is not simple-minded English. Utilise adds nothing to use. In order to is usually just to. Due to the fact that means because. Each cut makes the reader’s job a little easier.

Active and passive

In the active voice, the subject performs the action: “The committee approved the budget.” In the passive, the subject receives it: “The budget was approved by the committee.” The active is usually shorter and clearer, and it names who is responsible.

The passive has honest uses: when the doer is unknown (“The car was stolen”), unimportant (“The samples were stored at 4°C”) or obvious from context. Watch for its evasive use. “Mistakes were made” tells you nothing about who made them.

Vary the rhythm

A string of sentences of the same length becomes monotonous, whether they are all short or all long. Mix them. Use short sentences for emphasis. Let longer ones carry explanation, detail and the turns of an argument, and then follow them with a short one again.

Read a paragraph aloud. If you run out of breath, a sentence is too long. If it sounds like a telegram, it is too choppy.

Match the tone to the purpose

Before you write, ask three questions. Who is my reader? What do I want them to do? How do I want them to feel?

A complaint wants action, so it should be firm, specific and calm, never angry. A condolence note wants to comfort, so it should be warm and brief. A report wants to be trusted, so it should be precise and neutral.

Good tone is invisible. The reader does not notice it. They simply feel respected, and they understand what to do next.
