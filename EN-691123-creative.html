Show, Don’t Tell: The Craft of Writing Stories in English
Creative writing breaks many of the rules of formal prose, but it has rules of its own. A growing shelf of Thai stories in English shows that the craft can be learned.

Not long ago, few Thai stories reached readers in English. That is changing. Rattawut Lapcharoensap’s story collection “Sightseeing” (2005) and Pitchaya Sudbanthad’s novel “Bangkok Wakes to Rain” (2019), both written in English, found international audiences. The translator Mui Poopoksakul has brought the work of Prabda Yoon and Duanwad Pimwana to English-language readers; Duanwad’s novel “Bright,” which won the S.E.A. Write Award in Thai in 2003, appeared in English in 2019.

These writers show that stories rooted in Thailand can speak to the world. The skills behind them can be learned by anyone willing to read closely and write often.

Show, don’t tell

The most repeated advice in creative writing is to show rather than tell. Telling states an emotion or a quality. Showing gives the reader the evidence and lets them reach the conclusion themselves.

Telling: Grandmother was very sad after Grandfather died.

Showing: Every morning, Grandmother still set out two cups for coffee. Every evening, she washed one of them, unused.

The second version never says “sad,” yet the reader feels it more. Readers enjoy the small work of inference, which makes the feeling their own.

Telling still has its place. Use it to move quickly through time (“Three years passed”), and save showing for the moments that matter.

Be specific

Vague words make vague pictures. “A tree” is weaker than “a rain tree,” and “food” is weaker than “a bag of sticky rice still warm from the steamer.” Specific details also anchor a story in place. A reader who has never been to Isan can almost smell a story set there if the writer names the right things: smoke from burning rice straw, the drone of cicadas, a tinny radio playing mor lam.

Use all five senses, not just sight. Sound, smell, touch and taste often bring a scene to life more vividly than any description of how it looks.

Choose a point of view

Decide who tells the story. First person (“I”) offers intimacy but limits the reader to what one character knows. Third person limited (“she”) follows one character closely while giving the writer a little more freedom. Third person omniscient moves among many minds. Whatever you choose, stay consistent within a scene. Jumping between characters’ thoughts in the middle of a paragraph confuses readers.

Tense matters, too. Most stories are told in the past tense, which feels natural to English readers; the present tense adds urgency. Whichever you choose, keep to it. Accidental shifts in tense are among the most common faults in stories by second-language writers, and Thai writers, whose own verbs do not mark tense, need to watch for them closely.

Write dialogue that sounds real

Good dialogue is not a transcript of real speech. It cuts the “ums” and the small talk, and keeps the tension. Characters rarely say exactly what they mean, and what they avoid saying can reveal more than what they say.

Punctuate dialogue carefully. Start a new paragraph for each new speaker, and put the punctuation inside the quotation marks: “I’m not hungry,” she said. Use said for most dialogue tags, because readers barely notice it. Fancier verbs such as exclaimed, retorted and opined draw attention to themselves and away from the story.

Learn from short forms

A six-word story, “For sale: baby shoes, never worn,” is often attributed to Ernest Hemingway, although there is no evidence that he wrote it. Whoever did shows how much a story can leave unsaid.

Hemingway did describe a related idea, which became known as his iceberg theory. In “Death in the Afternoon” (1932), he wrote that a writer who truly knows his subject may leave things out, and the reader will still feel them. The dignity of an iceberg’s movement, he said, comes from only one-eighth of it being above water.

Try flash fiction, a complete story in fewer than 1,000 words, or set yourself a limit of 100. Short forms teach economy, because every word must earn its place.

Read like a writer

Read stories, then read them again, asking how they work. How does the first sentence pull you in? Where does the writer show, and where tell? How does the ending echo the beginning? Copy out by hand a paragraph you admire; it slows you down enough to notice the writer’s choices.

Then write

Set a small daily goal, such as 300 words or 20 minutes. Write the first draft quickly and imperfectly; stories are made in revision. And let that draft be in your own voice. Thai experiences, rhythms and humour are not obstacles to writing in English. They are what will make your stories worth reading.
