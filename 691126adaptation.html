A Year After the Hat Yai Flood, Thailand Is Still Learning to Live With Water
The flood that devastated southern Thailand’s commercial hub last November exposed how unprepared cities are for the rain a warmer world delivers. A year on, the fixes are under way, but slowly.
Floodwater fills the streets of Hat Yai, Songkhla province, during the flood of November 2010. Last year’s flood dropped far more rain on the city.

A year ago this week, the sky opened over Hat Yai. On November 21, 2025, 335 millimetres of rain fell on southern Thailand’s largest commercial city in a single day. Over three days, from November 19 to 21, the total reached about 630 millimetres, far more than the 428 millimetres that caused the city’s memorable flood of 2010.

The water rose faster than anyone had planned for. By November 29, at least 162 people had died across eight southern provinces, 126 of them in Songkhla, according to figures reported at the time. Estimates of the damage still vary widely, from 25 billion baht by Kasikorn Research Center to 40 billion baht by the University of the Thai Chamber of Commerce.

The disaster also exposed failures of command. The government imposed an emergency decree on November 25, but the response was criticised for lacking a central command to direct rescues and resources, and residents received mixed messages about whether to evacuate. The principle is simple, even if the practice is not: in an emergency, one clear chain of command and one consistent message save lives.

Adaptation, in the language of climate policy, means preparing for the changes that warming has already made unavoidable. Hotter air holds more moisture, so the heaviest downpours are becoming heavier. Cities built across natural floodplains, as much of Hat Yai has been, feel the consequences first.

The fixes under way in Hat Yai show both the possibilities and the pace. Repairs to the Khlong R.1 drainage canal, budgeted at 400 million baht, were ordered by the government in August to be completed within 90 days. Broader drainage upgrades are scheduled for 2028 to 2030, and a 53-kilometre diversion canal is planned from 2031. A retention basin at Khlong Rian is to be enlarged from 530,000 to 780,000 cubic metres, and Kasetsart University is helping to build a machine-learning flood forecasting system. A proposed ring road of 12 to 13 billion baht that would double as a floodway has drawn criticism from experts.

Thailand’s wider framework exists on paper. Its National Adaptation Plan, covering 2023 to 2037, names six priority areas: water, agriculture and food security, tourism, public health, natural resources, and human settlements and security. A cell-broadcast system that can send warnings directly to every mobile phone in an affected area sent its first live alert in May 2025 and was tested nationwide this January. The harder task is making sure that warnings lead to timely action on the ground.

The capital is not immune. In late September, 200 to 300 millimetres of rain flooded eastern Bangkok. The Bangkok Metropolitan Administration has outlined a flood-defence plan of at least 100 billion baht over five to seven years, including six new drainage tunnels and 45 retention sites by 2027.

Water can also be too scarce. With a strong El Niño under way, Krungthai COMPASS, the research arm of Krungthai Bank, projected in June that farm losses could reach 62 billion baht, 43 billion of it in rice. In early June, dams in the central region held only 28 percent of their usable water. Adaptation means planning for drought and flood in the same year, sometimes in the same province.

Money for that work is hard to come by. At COP31 in Antalya, developing countries pressed for a firm commitment to triple adaptation finance by 2035, and left without one. For Thailand, that means most of the cost of adapting will fall on its own budgets and its own people.

Hat Yai has flooded badly before, notably in 2000 and 2010, and it will flood again. The lesson of last November is not that disasters can be prevented, but that the damage they do depends on choices made long before the rain begins: where we build, how we drain, and whether a warning reaches people in time to act.
