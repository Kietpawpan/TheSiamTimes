The Small Marks That Carry Big Meaning: A Guide to English Punctuation
Thai sentences end with a space. English sentences end with a mark, and the marks in between tell the reader how to read.

Open a Thai newspaper and you will find few full stops at the ends of sentences. Thai writing traditionally marks the end of a thought with a space rather than a mark, and Thai script has no capital letters. Readers rely on context to tell where one idea ends and the next begins.

English depends on punctuation instead. A capital letter announces a new sentence; a full stop closes it; commas, colons and dashes direct the traffic in between. When Thai writers carry the habit of space and context into English, the result is often a long chain of clauses joined only by commas, which English readers find hard to follow.

Here is a guide to the marks that matter most, with the rules that cover most situations.

The full stop

Known as the period in American English, the full stop ends every statement. If a sentence runs past three lines, check whether it is really two. Short sentences are not childish. They are clear.

With abbreviations, British style often drops the full stop when the short form ends with the word’s last letter (Mr, Dr), while American style keeps it (Mr., Dr.). Either is fine, as long as you are consistent.

The comma

The comma is the most used mark in English, and the most misused. Its main jobs are to separate items in a list, to follow an introductory phrase, to set off extra information and, together with and, but, or, so, yet, for or nor, to join two complete sentences. A comma alone cannot join two complete sentences. That error, the comma splice, heads our separate guide to “10 Common Comma Mistakes.”

The semicolon

A semicolon joins two complete sentences that are closely related: “The rain stopped; the streets were still flooded.” It also comes before words such as however and therefore when they link two sentences: “The data were incomplete; however, the trend was clear.”

Its second job is to separate items in a list when the items already contain commas: “The team visited Khon Kaen, in the northeast; Lampang, in the north; and Trang, in the south.”

If in doubt, use a full stop instead. A semicolon is never required, and it is often overused.

The colon

A colon promises that what follows will explain, list or illustrate what came before: “The plan has three parts: prevention, response and recovery.” The words before a colon should form a complete sentence. “The parts are: prevention, response and recovery” is a common error. Drop the colon.

The apostrophe

Apostrophes do two jobs. They show possession (the director’s office, the students’ results), and they mark missing letters in contractions (don’t, it’s). They never make a plural. A sign reading “Fresh mango’s” should read “Fresh mangoes.”

The most famous trap is its and it’s. It’s always means “it is” or “it has.” Its is possessive, like his and her, and takes no apostrophe: “The company changed its name.”

For a plural noun ending in s, put the apostrophe after the s: “the workers’ union.” For a plural that does not end in s, add ’s: “the children’s books.”

Quotation marks

Use quotation marks for direct speech and for words quoted from a source. American style uses double marks and puts commas and full stops inside them: “It’s late,” she said. British style often uses single marks and places other punctuation according to sense. Choose one style and keep to it throughout a document.

Hyphens and dashes

The hyphen (-) joins words: a well-known writer, a five-year plan. A compound adjective before a noun usually takes a hyphen; after the noun it usually does not. Compare “a well-written report” with “the report is well written.”

The en dash (–), slightly longer, marks ranges: 2020–2026, pages 10–15. The em dash (—), longer still, marks a sharp break or an aside — like this one. American publications usually close it up (word—word), while many British publications prefer a spaced en dash (word – word). In informal writing, a spaced hyphen is an acceptable stand-in when your keyboard lacks the proper marks.

Question and exclamation marks

Every direct question needs a question mark, including polite requests phrased as questions: “Could you send the file?” An indirect question does not: “She asked whether the file was ready.”

Use exclamation marks sparingly in formal writing. One per document is plenty, and none is often better. On Thai social media, rows of exclamation marks and a string of 555 (“hahaha,” because the Thai word for five is pronounced “ha”) add warmth. In an English business email, they can look unprofessional.

Capital letters

Because Thai has no capitals, English capitalisation needs deliberate practice. Capitalise the first word of a sentence; the pronoun I; the names of people, places and organisations; days and months; nationalities and languages (Thai, English, Japanese); and the main words of titles.

Do not capitalise ordinary nouns for emphasis. “Our Company values its Customers” should be “Our company values its customers.”

A final check

Read your writing aloud. A pause often signals where a comma belongs; a full stop is where your voice falls and stops. Then read it once more, looking only at the punctuation. A few minutes of checking can save your reader from a misunderstanding, and save you from having to explain what you meant.
