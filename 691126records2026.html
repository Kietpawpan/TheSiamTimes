2026 Set to Break Temperature Records as a Historic El Niño Builds
November has brought the largest daily temperature anomalies ever measured, and the race with 2024 for the hottest year on record is now too close to call. Forecasters expect 2027 to be hotter still.

On Monday, November 23, the planet’s average surface air temperature stood about 2.2°C above its pre-industrial level. That was the largest departure for any single day since the Copernicus Climate Change Service’s ERA5 record begins in 1940, according to an analysis by The Siam Times of the agency’s daily data. It capped a run of five consecutive days, from November 19 to 23, each of which beat the previous high, a mark that had stood since November 2023.

Those extraordinary days have turned this year’s race for the warmest year on record into a dead heat. From January 1 to November 24, 2026 averaged 1.56°C above pre-industrial levels in the Copernicus data. The current record holder, 2024, finished at 1.60°C. To draw level, the remaining 37 days of the year must average about 1.94°C. So far, November has averaged about 2°C. If that pace holds through December, 2026 would match 2024 to within a hundredth of a degree, a margin smaller than the data can reliably separate.

Copernicus itself, before November’s surge, put the odds of a tie or a new record at about one in five, and called it virtually certain that 2026 would end above 1.5°C. Other datasets are more decisive. Berkeley Earth, an independent research group in California, estimated on November 19 that there was a 97 percent chance 2026 would be the warmest year in its record, which stretches back to 1850. An analysis by Carbon Brief, a climate science website, judged this year effectively tied with 2024 when the major datasets are averaged together.

Whatever the final ranking, the year has already broken records of its own. Copernicus reported the warmest August and the warmest October on record, and average sea-surface temperatures set a record for every month from June to October. America’s National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration found that October had the largest temperature anomaly of any month in its record.

The immediate cause is El Niño, the periodic warming of the eastern tropical Pacific that releases heat into the atmosphere. American forecasters declared an El Niño on June 11. By mid-November, its key index stood about 2.5°C above normal, and the US Climate Prediction Center gave roughly a 60 percent chance that this would become a “historic” event. The World Meteorological Organization has said it may become the strongest El Niño on record by December.

El Niño is the accelerator, but greenhouse gases are the engine. In its update for the COP31 climate summit, the WMO said the years from 2015 to 2026 would be the 12 warmest on record, and 2023 to 2026 the four warmest. Because El Niño’s effect on global temperatures usually peaks in the year after it begins, 2027 is widely expected to be hotter still. Carbon Brief’s projection puts next year at around 1.74°C, comfortably above anything yet recorded.

Thailand has already felt the heat. In April, the Thai Meteorological Department recorded 43.5°C at Thoen district in Lampang, in a month with rainfall 51 percent below normal. Berkeley Earth reports that Thailand, Cambodia, Laos, Myanmar, the Philippines and Vietnam all set national records for average October temperature this year.

El Niño years have often brought weaker rains and a harsher dry season to Thailand, as in the severe drought of 2015 and 2016. With a powerful event under way, water managers, farmers and public health officials have good reason to prepare now for the dry months of early 2027: conserving reservoir water, planning crop calendars around a short supply, and readying heat warnings for the elderly, outdoor workers and the rural Northeast.

Copernicus will publish its verdict on 2026 in early January. Whether this year edges past 2024 or falls just short, the more important number may be the next one.
