Bottom Line First: The Habits That Make Business English Work
In meetings, reports and negotiations, clear English saves time and money. For many Thai professionals, the challenge is less about vocabulary than about directness.

In a Thai office, a considerate colleague practises kreng jai (เกรงใจ), the habit of sparing others inconvenience or embarrassment. It shapes how bad news is delivered, how requests are made and how disagreement is expressed: gently, indirectly and often not in public at all.

International business English runs on a different principle. Busy readers want the point first. The US military even has a name for it: BLUF, or “bottom line up front.” Put the conclusion, decision or request in the first sentence, then explain.

The challenge for Thai professionals is to be direct without losing courtesy. It can be done.

Lead with the point

Compare two openings of an email to a manager:

Following our discussion last week, I have been looking into various options for a new supplier and have spoken with several companies, considering factors such as price, delivery times and quality…

I recommend that we sign with Supplier B. It is 8 percent cheaper than our current supplier and can deliver within five days. The details are below.

The second respects the reader’s time. If they read only one sentence, they know what you want.

Disagree politely

Direct does not mean rude. English has ready-made phrases for disagreeing without giving offence:

I see your point, but I’m concerned that…

That’s a fair suggestion. Could we also consider…?

I’m not sure that would work, because…

Each phrase acknowledges the other person before it challenges the idea. They let you say no without saying “No.”

In meetings with international partners, silence is often read as agreement. If you have a concern, raise it in the meeting. A quiet nod today can become an awkward email next week.

Get the numbers right

Numbers are where business English most often goes wrong, and the mistakes can be expensive.

Thai counts in units that English does not have. A แสน is 100,000, so “สองแสนบาท” is 200,000 baht. A ล้าน is a million. When converting, write the full figure and check it twice. For large amounts, “1.25 million baht” is easier to read than “1,250,000 baht.”

Thailand writes numbers the same way English does, with commas for thousands and a point for decimals: 1,250,000.50. Many European countries use a comma as the decimal mark instead, so “1,5” from a partner in Germany or France means one and a half.

Dates need care, too. Thai official documents use the Buddhist Era, which runs 543 years ahead of the Western calendar: 2569 B.E. is 2026. Do not send an international partner a contract dated 2569 without explanation. Write dates in words to avoid confusion, since 3/4/2026 means 3 April in Thailand and Britain but March 4 in the United States. “3 April 2026” is unambiguous.

The Thai government’s fiscal year runs from 1 October to 30 September and is named for the year in which it ends, so fiscal 2026 began in October 2025. Say so when you write to partners who work on a calendar year.

Write reports that get read

A business report is not a story told in order. Start with an executive summary of no more than half a page: the purpose, the main findings and the recommendation. Use headings that say something. “Sales fell 12 percent in the north” is better than “Regional sales.” Put detailed tables in an appendix.

Prefer verbs to nouns. “We conducted an investigation into the matter” becomes “We investigated.” “Implementation of the system will take place in May” becomes “We will introduce the system in May.”

Handle the jargon

Business English is full of idioms: touch base (make brief contact), circle back (return to a topic later), ballpark figure (rough estimate) and on the same page (in agreement). Learn to understand them, because colleagues will use them.

Use them sparingly in your own writing, though, especially for readers whose first language is not English. A partner in Tokyo or Hamburg may find “Let’s circle back” as puzzling as you once did. The safest business English is international English: plain, literal and free of idioms that only make sense in New York or London.

Presentations and small talk

In a presentation, tell the audience what you will cover, cover it, then summarise. Keep to one message per slide. Spoken numbers are easy to mishear, since “thirteen” and “thirty” sound alike, so put key figures on the slide as well.

Small talk matters, too. Before a meeting, international colleagues often chat about travel, the weather or food, and Thai food is always a safe and welcome topic. Questions about age, salary, marital status or weight, common enough in Thai conversation, are best avoided.

The bottom line

Good business English is clear, brief and courteous. It puts the reader first: their time, their question, their next step. That principle translates into every culture, including our own. After all, saving a busy colleague’s time is its own form of kreng jai.
