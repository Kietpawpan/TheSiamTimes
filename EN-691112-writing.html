The Written Word Still Matters in an Age of AI
Learning to write well is still our best way to pass on knowledge, and to make good use of the machines that learned from us.
A vintage typewriter beside a modern smartphone. Both depend on the same ancient technology: written language. THE SIAM TIMES

We live in an age dazzled by science and technology. Yet much of it rests on an invention thousands of years old: writing. Writing lets knowledge outlive the people who discovered it, whether in a lab report or a history of empires. It is also, quite literally, what today’s AI is made of. Large language models learned their fluency from vast amounts of human prose.

What separates effective writing from mere typing? Good writing tends to share four qualities: it is correct, clear, concise and compelling. Prose that reads effortlessly is rarely written that way. It starts with the fundamentals: a broad vocabulary, a feel for how sentences are built and a sense of how paragraphs move. Style comes later.

Those fundamentals make a visible difference. “At this point in time, it is our considered opinion that the project should be postponed” says nothing that “We think the project should wait” does not. Spotting the difference is a skill, and it can be learned.

A few tools help. A good dictionary is more than a place to check spellings; its example sentences show how words actually behave. A usage guide helps too. William Strunk Jr.’s “The Elements of Style,” first printed in 1918 and revised by E.B. White in 1959, remains the best known, with its famous command to “omit needless words.” It has critics: linguists such as Geoffrey Pullum argue that some of its grammar rules are simply wrong. But its core lesson holds. Cut whatever does not earn its place.

Today the toolkit includes the technology that writing helped create. AI can check the tone of a delicate email, flag a muddled paragraph and tidy a rough draft. That raises a fair question: if a machine can polish our prose, why learn the craft at all?

Because editing takes judgment. An AI can offer ten versions of a sentence, but only a writer who knows what good writing looks like can pick the right one, or notice when all ten miss the point. Without that judgment, we cannot tell a sharp edit from a bland one, or a fluent sentence from a true one. The better we write, the better we use the tools.

That judgment, along with taste and voice, comes from reading and writing. To write well about the world, read widely about it and notice how good writers build their sentences. Then sit down and write, often and without excuses. The mechanics can be learned. What you do with them is up to you.
