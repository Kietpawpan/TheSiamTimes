The 1.5°C Line Is No Longer a Distant Horizon
The latest science says human-caused warming will cross the world’s most famous climate threshold around the end of this decade. The question now is how far we overshoot it, and for how long.

For a decade, one number has organised the world’s climate politics. Written into the Paris Agreement in 2015, which commits governments to pursue efforts to limit warming to 1.5°C above pre-industrial levels, it became a slogan on protest banners, a line in corporate reports and a quick test of whether a country was serious. In 2026, the meaning of that number is changing. It is no longer a goal to be defended so much as a threshold being crossed.

The clearest evidence comes from Indicators of Global Climate Change, an annual stocktake by an international team of scientists. Its latest edition, published in June, estimates that human activity had warmed the planet by 1.37°C by 2025, and that warming is proceeding at about 0.27°C per decade, as fast as at any time in the instrumental record. At that pace, human-caused warming reaches 1.5°C around 2030.

It is worth being precise about what that means. The Paris limit refers to warming averaged over many years, not to a single hot one. So when 2024 came in at 1.60°C above the pre-industrial level in the European Copernicus record, the target was not, by itself, broken. But the cushion is almost gone. Copernicus puts long-term warming at about 1.4°C, and 2023 to 2025 became the first three-year stretch to average more than 1.5°C.

The arithmetic of carbon tells the same story. To keep an even chance of holding warming to 1.5°C, that stocktake calculates, the world could emit only about 130 billion tonnes of carbon dioxide from the start of 2026. That is little more than three years of emissions at today’s rate. The Global Carbon Project, using a different method and starting point, puts the figure at about 150 billion tonnes, or under four years.

Emissions are not yet falling fast enough to change that conclusion. The Global Carbon Project’s annual report, released this month, projects that carbon dioxide from fossil fuels will dip by 0.4 percent in 2026, to 38.2 billion tonnes, largely because of a fall in oil use. Emissions from coal are still rising. Counting deforestation and other changes in land use, total carbon dioxide emissions are expected to climb to 43.3 billion tonnes, and the concentration of the gas in the atmosphere to average a record 427.7 parts per million.

In October, the UN Environment Programme drew the obvious conclusion. Its annual Emissions Gap Report found that current policies put the world on course for about 2.8°C of warming this century, and that even full delivery of every national pledge would leave it near 2.6°C. Avoiding any overshoot of 1.5°C, it said, is no longer possible. Bringing temperatures back down to 1.5°C by 2100 would require cutting global emissions by 18 percent by 2030 and 45 percent by 2035, compared with 2025.

Overshoot is not a technicality. It means living above the line for decades, and then relying on the large-scale removal of carbon dioxide from the air, a set of technologies still in their infancy, to bring temperatures back down. Every tenth of a degree in between brings more intense heat, heavier downpours and greater stress on coral reefs, crops and people.

For Thailand, the global average hides a harsher local reality, because land warms faster than the ocean. The country’s highest recorded temperature, 45.4°C, was measured in Tak in April 2023. This April, the Thai Meteorological Department logged 43.5°C at Thoen district in Lampang, in a month when rainfall was 51 percent below normal. The Department of Disease Control counted 21 heat-related deaths in 2025, more than half of them in the Northeast.

None of this makes 1.5°C meaningless. It remains the most useful yardstick for how much damage the world is choosing to accept, and every fraction of a degree avoided still counts. But the honest question has shifted. It is no longer whether the line will be crossed, but by how much, and for how long.

The stripes on this page will keep turning a deeper red for some years yet. Whether they ever fade back towards pink depends on decisions made in this decade, in Bangkok as much as in Beijing, Brussels or Washington.
