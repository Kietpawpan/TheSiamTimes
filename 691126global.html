In Antalya, the World’s Climate Talks Ran Out of Road
COP31 closed two days late without its central decision, as disputes over fossil fuels, finance and the 1.5°C limit sank the proposed “Antalya Pact”. Climate-vulnerable countries such as Thailand cannot afford a repeat.

The 31st United Nations climate summit was due to end on Friday, November 20. It finally closed at 7:33 p.m. on Sunday, November 22, more than two days late, and without the agreement that was supposed to define it.

In the final plenary in Antalya, Türkiye, the summit presidency put forward the “Antalya Pact”, a pair of political cover decisions under the UN climate convention and the Paris Agreement. Several countries refused to accept it. With consensus impossible, the texts were not adopted.

According to the Earth Negotiations Bulletin, which has reported on UN climate talks for more than three decades, the objections centred on three related failings: an inadequate response to the science and to the overshoot of 1.5°C, the absence of any language on transitioning away from fossil fuels, and insufficient finance for developing countries. The European Union formally objected to the cover decision. Draft decisions on the work programme meant to raise emission-cutting ambition, on response measures and on guidance to the Global Environment Facility were also rejected.

The collapse exposed a structural feature of the UN process. Decisions are taken by consensus, so a single objection can stop a text that most countries support. The rule protects small and vulnerable states from being outvoted by larger powers, but it also lets any party block progress, and it rewards wording vague enough for everyone to accept. In Antalya, the objections came from parties that judged the text too weak, which shows that consensus can be used to demand more ambition as well as to resist it.

The summit was unusual from the start. Türkiye and Australia had both bid to host it, and under a compromise struck last year, Türkiye held the presidency while Australia led the negotiations. With 92,934 people registered to attend in person, it was among the largest climate conferences ever held.

Not everything collapsed. Negotiators extended the work programme on a just transition for three more years and agreed to set up a just transition mechanism in phases, with adoption expected at next year’s summit. They launched the second global stocktake of climate action, which will conclude at COP33 in November 2028, and advanced work on the 59 indicators adopted in Belém last year to measure progress on adaptation.

Money was the sharpest point of pain. The fund created to help vulnerable countries cope with loss and damage from climate disasters had received only $485.77 million of the $822.71 million pledged to it by the end of July, a gap the summit noted “with concern”. Developing countries left Antalya without a clear plan for meeting the goal, set in Belém, of at least tripling finance for adaptation by 2035.

Behind the deadlock lies a wider ambition gap. A UN synthesis of the new national climate plans submitted by 154 countries by the end of August found that, together, they would cut emissions only about 6 percent below 2019 levels by 2035, or about 9 percent if fully carried out. According to the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change, cuts of around 60 percent are needed in that time to keep 1.5°C within reach. The United States, the largest historical emitter, sent no official delegation; it left the Paris Agreement in January and its withdrawal from the climate convention itself takes effect in February 2027.

Yet the physics of climate change does not wait for diplomatic agreement. Greenhouse gases accumulate whether or not a cover decision is adopted, and every year of delay narrows the options that remain for staying anywhere near 1.5°C.

For Thailand, which ranks among the countries most exposed to floods, drought and extreme heat, the cost of delay is concrete. The money for adaptation and for loss and damage that Antalya failed to unlock is precisely what flood-prone provinces and drought-stricken farmers will need as warming accelerates.

The next summit, COP32, is scheduled for Addis Ababa, Ethiopia, in November 2027. Until then, the real test of climate action will take place at home, in national energy plans, budgets and laws, where no single objection can block progress.
