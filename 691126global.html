In Antalya, the World’s Climate Talks Ran Out of Road
COP31 closed two days late without its central decision, as disputes over fossil fuels, finance and the 1.5°C limit sank the proposed “Antalya Pact”. Climate-vulnerable countries such as Thailand cannot afford a repeat.

The 31st United Nations climate summit was due to end on Friday, November 20. It finally closed at 7:33 p.m. on Sunday, November 22, more than two days late, and without the agreement that was supposed to define it.

In the final plenary in Antalya, Türkiye, the summit’s president, Murat Kurum, put forward the “Antalya Pact”, a pair of political cover decisions under the UN climate convention and the Paris Agreement. Several countries refused to accept it. With consensus impossible, the texts fell. “I understand that there is no agreement on this text,” Mr. Kurum told delegates. “For that reason, these decisions have not been adopted.”

According to the Earth Negotiations Bulletin, which has reported on UN climate talks for more than three decades, the objections centred on three related failings: an inadequate response to the science and to the overshoot of 1.5°C, the absence of any language on transitioning away from fossil fuels, and insufficient finance for developing countries. The European Union formally objected to the cover decision. Draft decisions on the work programme meant to raise emission-cutting ambition, on response measures and on guidance to the Global Environment Facility were also rejected.

The summit was unusual from the start. Türkiye and Australia had both bid to host it, and under a compromise struck last year, Türkiye held the presidency while Australia, through its climate minister Chris Bowen, led the negotiations as “President of Negotiations”. With 92,934 people registered to attend in person, it was among the largest climate conferences ever held.

Not everything collapsed. Negotiators extended the work programme on a just transition for three more years and agreed to set up a just transition mechanism in phases, with adoption expected at next year’s summit. They launched the second global stocktake of climate action, which will conclude at COP33 in November 2028, and advanced work on the 59 indicators adopted in Belém last year to measure progress on adaptation.

Simon Stiell, the UN’s climate chief, did not hide his frustration. “I share the deep disappointment of many that COP31 has been unable to agree on issues related to the Presidency-Led Decision,” he said, adding that “there are lessons that must be learnt.” He insisted the process was still worth fighting for: “The fight continues. We will never give up.” Elsewhere he put it more bluntly: “The world cannot afford a repeat of this moment.”

Money was the sharpest point of pain. The fund created to help vulnerable countries cope with loss and damage from climate disasters had received only $485.77 million of the $822.71 million pledged to it by the end of July, a gap the summit noted “with concern”. Developing countries left Antalya without a clear plan for meeting the goal, set in Belém, of at least tripling finance for adaptation by 2035.

Behind the deadlock lies a wider ambition gap. A UN synthesis of the new national climate plans submitted by 154 countries by the end of August found that, together, they would cut emissions only about 6 percent below 2019 levels by 2035, or about 9 percent if fully carried out. Scientists say cuts of around 60 percent are needed in that time to keep 1.5°C within reach. The United States, the largest historical emitter, sent no official delegation; it left the Paris Agreement in January and its withdrawal from the climate convention itself takes effect in February 2027.

UN Secretary-General António Guterres offered the summit’s most quotable verdict. “There may be no consensus in the negotiating room,” he said. “There is no ambiguity in the science.”

For Thailand, which ranks among the countries most exposed to floods, drought and extreme heat, that is the point. A stalled negotiation does not pause the weather. The money for adaptation and for loss and damage that Antalya failed to unlock is precisely what flood-prone provinces and drought-stricken farmers will need as warming accelerates.

The next summit, COP32, is scheduled for Addis Ababa, Ethiopia, in November 2027. Until then, the real test of climate action will take place at home, in national energy plans, budgets and laws, where no single objection can block progress.
