A Thai Sprinter’s Path to 9.90 Seconds Was Paved With Patience and Steel
Puripon Boonson’s historic gold at the Asian Games was a triumph of mechanical precision over high-tech flash, forged through a partnership with a coach he once questioned.

Puripon Boonson, left, and his guess, at the Athletic Association of Thailand in Bangkok. PERSPECTIVE

In the sweltering humidity of Bangkok, at a training facility that feels more like a museum of track and field than a laboratory for modern speed, Puripon Boonson found the mechanical key to his own potential. At 20 years old, Mr. Boonson has become the face of a new era for Thai athletics, clocking a blistering 9.90 seconds to secure gold at the Asian Games and shatter the elusive 10-second barrier. It was a performance that announced his arrival on the world stage, yet the partnership that produced it was initially defined by profound skepticism.

When Gentry Bradley, a coach who approaches sprinting with the meticulous eye of an automotive mechanic, first took over Mr. Boonson’s training, the young prodigy was unconvinced. Mr. Bradley’s own personal bests on the track were slower than the times Mr. Boonson was already recording. "Personally, he was slower than me when he ran," Mr. Boonson recalled. "So I wondered, can he really make me faster?" Mr. Bradley, undeterred, began the work of dismantling Mr. Boonson’s stride, looking for where power was being leaked and efficiency lost. He preached a gospel of patience, urging his pupil to slow down at the start to master the movement before adding the raw octane of his natural talent.

The breakthrough arrived the day the stopwatch hit 9.90. For Mr. Bradley, it was the moment the athlete’s doubt evaporated, replaced by a total conversion to his method. That method is rooted in a two-way street of motivation: Mr. Boonson’s relentless drive to be the best pushes his coach to refine the program further, asking constantly how a human engine can be tuned for even greater velocity.

Perhaps most striking is the environment where this speed is manufactured. The Athletic Association of Thailand under Royal Patronage does not boast the gleaming chrome or digital sensors of Western athletic powerhouses. Instead, the weight room is filled with seasoned equipment that has seen decades of use. To Mr. Boonson, these tools are not outdated; they are seasoned by the legacy of the champions who came before him. By maintaining its older gear rather than pursuing a cycle of constant replacement, the association has turned resource efficiency into a masterclass of grit. In a world of high-tech marginal gains, Mr. Boonson and Mr. Bradley have proven that historic results can still be forged with raw talent, a mechanic’s patience, and a very old set of weights.
