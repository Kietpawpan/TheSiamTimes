Before You Hit Send: The Unwritten Rules of Email Etiquette
In a country where daily work runs on LINE, email remains the language of formal business. A few habits decide whether a message gets answered or ignored.

In Thailand, much day-to-day work now happens on LINE. Quick questions, photos of documents and meeting reminders fly back and forth in group chats. But when a message is formal, goes abroad or needs a record, email is still the standard. And email has rules that chat does not.

Write a subject line that works

The subject line is the first thing your reader sees, and often the only thing they use to decide whether to open your message now or later. Make it specific.

Weak: Meeting

Better: Budget meeting moved to Thursday, 10 a.m.

Weak: Question

Better: Request for 2025 water-quality data, Lam Takhong River

If you need action by a deadline, say so in the subject line: “Action needed by 30 November: signed contract.”

Greet your reader correctly

The greeting sets the tone. “Dear” followed by a name is safe for formal and first-time emails: “Dear Dr Smith,” “Dear Ms Johnson.” “Hello” and “Hi” suit colleagues you know. Avoid “Dear Sir/Madam” when you can find a name; it feels impersonal.

Names are where many Thai writers slip. In Thai, a title goes with a person’s first name, as in คุณสมชาย. In English, a title such as Mr, Ms or Dr goes with the family name. “Dear Mr John” should be “Dear Mr Smith,” or simply “Dear John” if you are on first-name terms.

The reverse applies to Thai colleagues. “Dear Khun Somchai” is respectful and widely understood in English emails. International partners may not know that Thai people are addressed by their first names, so a short note can save them an awkward “Dear Mr Jaidee.” Keep nicknames for informal messages.

One email, one topic

Keep each email to a single subject. If you need a decision on the budget and an update on recruitment, send two emails with two clear subject lines. They are easier to answer, forward and find later.

Put your main point or request in the first two lines; busy readers may not scroll down. Keep paragraphs short, two to four sentences, and number your questions so that the reader can answer each one in turn.

Mind the tone

Without a face or a voice, emails are easily misread. Short replies can seem cold, and capital letters read as shouting. Before sending, reread your message as if you were the recipient on a bad day.

Be polite without being stiff. “Could you please send the report by Friday?” works better than both “Send the report by Friday” and “I would be most grateful if you could kindly consider sending the report at your earliest convenience.”

Avoid “ASAP,” which can sound demanding; give a date instead. And be careful with humour and sarcasm, which rarely survive the journey.

Use CC and BCC wisely

The To line is for people who need to act. CC, or carbon copy, is for people who need to know. BCC, or blind carbon copy, hides recipients from one another. Use it for large mailing lists to protect people’s addresses, but never to secretly copy someone into a conversation.

Protecting addresses is more than good manners. Email addresses count as personal data under Thailand’s Personal Data Protection Act, so exposing a whole customer list in the To line can create real problems.

Think twice before you click “Reply All.” Does everyone on the thread need your “Thanks!”? Usually not.

Handle attachments with care

Mention attachments in the body of your email (“I have attached the revised schedule”) and check that they are actually attached before you send. Name files clearly: “Budget_FY2027_v2.pdf” is more useful than “scan001.pdf.” Send a PDF when you do not want a document edited, and share large files through a link rather than as attachments.

“Please find attached” is still common, but it sounds a little old-fashioned. “I have attached” or “Attached is” works just as well.

Close with care

“Best regards” and “Kind regards” are safe in almost any situation; “Best” and “Thanks” suit colleagues you know well. “Yours sincerely” and “Yours faithfully” belong to formal letters.

Add a signature with your full name, job title, organisation and phone number. Keep it short. Nobody needs an inspirational quote of the day.

Reply in good time

Aim to reply within one working day, even if only to say, “Thanks, I’ll get back to you by Wednesday.” A short acknowledgement is better than silence.

“Noted with thanks,” common in offices across Asia, is fine for internal mail. To international readers, “Thank you, noted” or “Thanks, I’ll take care of it” sounds more natural.

The final check

Before you hit send, ask five questions. Is the recipient correct? Is the subject line specific? Is the request clear? Are the attachments there? Is the tone right? A minute of checking can save an hour of follow-up.

One last trick: add the recipient’s address only after you have finished writing and checking. That way, you cannot send the message by accident before it is ready.
