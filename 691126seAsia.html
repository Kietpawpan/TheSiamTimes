Southeast Asia’s Data Centre Boom Is Racing Ahead of Its Clean Power
Tens of billions of dollars in server farms are heading for Johor, Batam and Thailand’s Eastern Seaboard. Whether they run on sunshine or on coal and gas will shape the region’s climate future.

In May, Thailand’s Board of Investment approved three data centre projects worth a combined 913 billion baht, about $27 billion. The largest, an 842-billion-baht expansion by TikTok, will add servers and data processing across Bangkok, Samut Prakan and Chachoengsao. A 200-megawatt campus in Chachoengsao and a 134-megawatt site in Chonburi made up the rest.

At the same meeting, the board did something that would once have seemed out of place at an investment agency. It discussed with energy officials how to secure urgent power supplies for incoming projects, particularly in the Eastern region, and urged faster issuance of Thailand’s long-delayed Power Development Plan. It also pushed new rules that would let companies buy renewable electricity directly from producers.

The scene is being repeated across the region. In Malaysia, projections reported in October put data centres’ peak power demand on the peninsula at 8.39 gigawatts by 2030, more than half of it in the southern state of Johor, along with demand for 389 million litres of water a day in Johor and Selangor. Across the strait in Batam, Indonesia, a hyperscale operator signed a deal in April for about 450 megawatts of power from the state utility.

The difficulty is the grid these servers will plug into. According to the energy think tank Ember, coal and gas generated more than 73 percent of the electricity in the Association of Southeast Asian Nations in 2025, while solar and wind supplied only around 5 percent. Every new megawatt of round-the-clock demand risks being met by the fuels that are already there.

The region’s plans to retire coal have proved fragile. Indonesia has pledged to phase out coal power by 2040, yet a planned early retirement of the Cirebon-1 coal plant in West Java, once a flagship of international climate finance, was cancelled last December over the cost of compensation. Indonesia’s Just Energy Transition Partnership, from which the United States withdrew in March 2025, had disbursed $3.93 billion of $21.4 billion by August, according to the Indonesian government.

One answer is to share clean power across borders. Ember estimates that every year the long-planned ASEAN Power Grid slips past its 2035 target costs the region up to $2.6 billion, and a five-year delay almost $14 billion. A working example already exists: the Laos-Thailand-Malaysia-Singapore power integration project, whose second phase, signed in January, allows up to 200 megawatts of electricity to flow from Laos to Singapore.

The stakes are not abstract. A year ago this month, floods in Sumatra after Cyclone Senyar killed 1,178 people, according to Indonesia’s disaster agency. Typhoons battered the Philippines and Vietnam in the same weeks. Southeast Asia is among the regions most exposed to the warming that its own power stations are adding to.

There are reasons for cautious hope. Large technology companies have their own net-zero targets and are among the most willing buyers of renewable power in the world. If governments let them buy it, through direct power purchase agreements, green tariffs and rooftop solar, data centres could become the anchor customers that finance new wind and solar farms. The same Thai investment board meeting approved an 89-megawatt wind farm in Nakhon Ratchasima.

The servers are coming either way. The choice before Southeast Asia’s energy planners, in the next two or three years, is whether they arrive as a reason to build clean power or as an excuse to burn more fossil fuel for decades to come.
