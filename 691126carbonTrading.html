The Economics of Carbon Trading: What Is a Tonne of Pollution Worth?
From about €80 in Europe to a few dollars in voluntary markets, the price of carbon varies enormously around the world. As Thailand prepares its own emissions trading system, the gap matters more than ever.

Economists have rarely agreed on anything as firmly as they agree on this: greenhouse gas pollution is a cost that polluters do not pay. A factory that burns coal pays for the coal, but not for the floods, heatwaves and failed harvests its emissions help cause. Put a price on that damage, the theory goes, and markets will find the cheapest ways to cut it.

There are two main ways to set that price. A carbon tax fixes the price and lets the quantity of emissions adjust. An emissions trading system, or cap-and-trade, does the reverse. The government sets a cap on total emissions and issues allowances up to that limit. Companies that can cut pollution cheaply sell their spare allowances to those for whom cuts are expensive, and the market price that emerges reveals what the next tonne of reductions costs. Each year, the cap tightens.

By the World Bank’s latest count, published in May, 87 carbon taxes and trading systems now cover just over 29 percent of global greenhouse gas emissions. They raised more than $107 billion in 2025. Yet the average price was only about $21 a tonne, far below most estimates of the damage a tonne causes. Before the current administration abandoned the measure, the United States Environmental Protection Agency had put that damage at $190 a tonne.

Europe’s trading system remains the benchmark. Its allowance price swung this year from about €93 in January to below €70 in February, amid uncertainty over reforms, and stood in the low €80s this week. From January 2026, the European Union’s Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism moved into its definitive phase. Importers bringing in 50 tonnes or more a year of cement, iron and steel, aluminium or fertilisers must now account for the carbon embedded in those goods, and from February 2027 they will start buying certificates to cover it.

The border mechanism is designed to stop “carbon leakage”, the shifting of dirty production to countries without a carbon price. But it also has a subtler effect. Importers can deduct carbon prices already paid in the country of production. A tonne priced in Thailand is a tonne on which Thailand collects the revenue; a tonne left unpriced may be charged at the European border instead.

That logic is spreading across Asia. China’s national market, the world’s largest by emissions covered, added steel, cement and aluminium to the power sector last year, with prices averaging about 84 yuan, roughly $12, a tonne in the first eight months of 2026. Japan’s trading system became mandatory for large emitters in April. Singapore’s carbon tax rose to S$45 a tonne this year, and Vietnam is piloting a market for power, steel and cement plants. Malaysia, by contrast, postponed its planned carbon tax in April.

Thailand sits in the middle. In January 2025, the cabinet approved building a carbon price of 200 baht a tonne of carbon dioxide into the excise tax on fuels, structured so that pump prices would not rise. A Climate Change Act that would create a national emissions trading system, a carbon tax and a border measure of Thailand’s own is still being reviewed by the Council of State, the government’s legal adviser. Officials expect it to take effect in 2027.

Then there are carbon credits, which work on different economics. Instead of capping emissions, they reward projects elsewhere, a protected forest or a cleaner cookstove, for reductions or removals. Prices in the voluntary market are low and falling: an MSCI index of credit prices fell to $3.50 a tonne in 2025, from $4.30 the year before. Doubts about whether many credits represent real reductions explain much of that weakness, and higher-rated credits fetch a clear premium. Removing carbon from the air with machines, by contrast, still costs several hundred dollars a tonne.

The gap between those numbers is the real lesson of carbon economics. A cheap credit is only a bargain if the reduction behind it is genuine, and a carbon price only changes behaviour if it is high enough to matter. For Thai policymakers drafting their own market, the hard choices lie in the design: how tight the cap, how many free allowances for heavy industry, how many offsets to allow, and whether the revenue helps households and small firms through the transition.

The question for Thai exporters is no longer whether their carbon will have a price. It is who will collect it.
