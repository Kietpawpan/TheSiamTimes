Loy Krathong, and What Each Generation Calls a Forest
As Thailand’s festival of lights drifts from riverbanks to park ponds to phone screens, the bigger question may be what the next generation will even recognise as Loy Krathong.
A family releases candlelit krathongs from a wooden pier on the night of Loy Krathong — a scene once common along Thailand’s rivers and canals.

If Songkran is the Thai festival the world knows best, Loy Krathong is surely a close second. On the full-moon night of the twelfth lunar month, usually in November, people in every region of Thailand set small floats called krathong onto the water. Traditionally shaped from slices of banana trunk and folded banana leaves, each one carries flowers, a candle, incense sticks and often a coin.

Yet the heart of the festival is not the beauty of candlelight on dark water. It is an apology. Each krathong is an offering to Phra Mae Khongkha, the goddess of water, asking forgiveness for a year of drinking from her, bathing in her and pouring our waste into her.

Old Siam was a land of rivers and canals. Houses stood at the water’s edge, and floating a krathong meant little more than walking down the steps of your own pier. Today most Thais live in cities, far from any riverbank, and many of Bangkok’s canals were long ago filled in to make roads.

So Loy Krathong has moved: to ponds in public parks, to lakes beside shopping malls, to water features in restaurants. What was once a private rite performed at home has become an event you go out to attend.

That shift has a cost. When thousands of people float their krathongs in the same place, the waste gathers in the same place too. The materials have changed as well. Banana trunk and leaf, which break down naturally, have often given way to polystyrene foam, which barely breaks down at all.

Every year, the morning after the festival, workers haul vast numbers of spent krathongs out of the water. A ceremony born to apologise to the river has become one more burden for the river to bear.

Growing environmental awareness has prompted change. Some people now choose not to float anything at all. Others use krathongs made of bread, which fish can eat, or of ice. In recent years, a new option has appeared: the digital krathong, launched with a tap on a screen.

A digital krathong leaves no rubbish behind. But it also has no scent of incense, no curl of candle smoke, no coin carefully tucked among the petals, no moment when your fingertips touch the water and let it go. To people of my generation, that feels like a loss. Younger Thais, born into an era when the festival had already faded in importance, may not feel that anything is missing at all.

I am hardly different. These days, when I see a small cluster of trees, I admire it as a “forest”. For earlier generations, a forest meant true tropical rainforest. Ecologists have a name for this: shifting baseline syndrome, in which each generation takes the world it was born into as normal and measures loss against that.

So perhaps the question worth asking is not which way of celebrating Loy Krathong is the right one. It is this: a generation from now, what will children who grew up floating krathongs on a screen call Loy Krathong?
