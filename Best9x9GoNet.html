The Master of the Small Board: A New Peak for Artificial Intelligence in Go
A specialized update for the KataGo engine demonstrates that even in the ancient game’s most compact form, there is still room for machines to find a sharper edge.
Digital representations of a 9-by-9 Go board, where the game’s strategic complexity is condensed into a claustrophobic space.

Since the ascent of AlphaGo, the world of professional Go has been dominated by neural networks trained to navigate the vast 361-intersection expanse of the traditional board. But a recent release by the developer known as lightvector suggests that for artificial intelligence, the smallest stages often require the most specialized training. The update, a "pre-release" neural network for the open-source engine KataGo, marks a significant departure from general-purpose models, offering a masterclass in the nuances of the nine-by-nine board.

The new model is the result of months of intensive fine-tuning, powered by a trio of high-end personal graphics processors. While KataGo has long been the gold standard for public Go AI, its primary versions are "generalists," trained on the massive data of standard 19-by-19 play. By narrowing the focus specifically to the nine-by-nine variant, the developer has achieved a level of precision that general models cannot match, reporting gains of up to 200 Elo—a standard measure of relative skill—in certain competitive scenarios.

To achieve this, the system was fed a curated diet of specialized data. This included everything from professional human matches and top-level bot games to the remnants of failed attempts to map out opening moves. Even the "blind spots" of previous versions were identified and addressed through rigorous sampling. The result is a network that understands the cramped, often lethal skirmishes of the small board with far greater efficiency than its predecessors, though the developer notes that at this elite level of play, the game increasingly edges toward a draw as both sides approach perfect play.

However, this newfound expertise comes with a distinct trade-off. The developer warns that this network should not be used for the traditional 19-by-19 game. Having spent months immersed in the geometry of the smaller board, the AI has essentially "forgotten" the grand strategic maneuvers and complex opening patterns, known as joseki, required for full-scale play. Its evaluation of larger boards has drifted, its capacity effectively repurposed for a different kind of excellence.

Beyond its competitive strength, the update includes experimental features that pique the curiosity of game theorists. One such test involves extreme "handicap" scenarios, where the AI attempts to survive against overwhelming odds. Preliminary data suggests that while the machine can find life when surrounded by four stones on the tiny board, a fifth stone may finally prove to be an insurmountable barrier—a rare glimpse into the mathematical limits of a game that continues to challenge both man and machine.
