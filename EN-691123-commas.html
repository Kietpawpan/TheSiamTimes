10 Common Comma Mistakes, and How to Fix Them
The comma is the most common punctuation mark in English and the most often misused. In one American court case, a missing comma cost a dairy company $5 million.

In 2017, a US federal appeals court sided with a group of delivery drivers in Maine who had sued their employer, Oakhurst Dairy, for overtime pay. The case turned on a single missing comma.

State law exempted from overtime “the canning, processing, preserving, freezing, drying, marketing, storing, packing for shipment or distribution of” certain foods. Did that mean “packing for shipment or for distribution,” a single activity the drivers did not do? Or was “distribution” a separate exempt task, which they did? With no comma after “shipment,” the court found the law ambiguous and ruled for the drivers. The dairy later settled for $5 million.

Few commas carry that kind of price. But misplaced commas confuse readers every day. Here are ten of the most common mistakes.

1. The comma splice

A comma cannot join two complete sentences on its own.

Wrong: The meeting ran late, we missed the train.

Right: The meeting ran late, so we missed the train.

A full stop or a semicolon would also work. This is the most common comma error of all, and especially common among Thai writers, because Thai links clauses with spaces and context rather than punctuation.

2. No comma after a long introduction

Put a comma after an introductory phrase or clause, especially a long one.

Wrong: After the minister finished her speech the reporters rushed to the stage.

Right: After the minister finished her speech, the reporters rushed to the stage.

After a short phrase (“In 2025 sales rose”), the comma is optional. After a long one, it prevents misreading.

3. A comma between subject and verb

Never separate a subject from its verb with a single comma, however long the subject is.

Wrong: The number of tourists visiting Khao Yai National Park in the cool season, has risen sharply.

Right: The number of tourists visiting Khao Yai National Park in the cool season has risen sharply.

This is one case where the “pause test” fails. You may pause when reading the sentence aloud, but the comma still does not belong.

4. No comma before a joining word

When and, but, or, so, yet, for or nor joins two complete sentences, put a comma before it.

Right: River levels fell, but the fields remained flooded.

If the second part is not a complete sentence, leave the comma out: “River levels fell but remained above normal.”

5. Commas around essential information

Commas mark information as extra. If the information is essential to identify what you mean, leave them out.

Students who cheat will be expelled. (Only the students who cheat.)

Students, who cheat, will be expelled. (All students cheat, and all will be expelled.)

The same logic governs that and which. In American English, that introduces essential clauses, without commas, and which introduces extra ones, with commas: “the report that I wrote” versus “the report, which I wrote in May.” British English also allows which in essential clauses, but the comma rule holds in both.

6. The forgotten second comma

Extra information in the middle of a sentence needs a comma on both sides.

Wrong: My sister, a nurse in Khon Kaen said the hospital was full.

Right: My sister, a nurse in Khon Kaen, said the hospital was full.

7. However, therefore and friends

Words such as however, therefore, moreover and consequently cannot join two sentences with just a comma.

Wrong: The project was approved, however the budget was cut.

Right: The project was approved; however, the budget was cut.

Also right: The project was approved, but the budget was cut.

8. The missing comma of direct address

When you speak to someone by name, separate the name with commas. “Let’s eat, Grandma!” is an invitation. “Let’s eat Grandma!” is a very different proposal.

The same applies in emails: “Thank you, Khun Malee.” Strictly, “Hi, John” takes a comma, too, although many writers now leave it out of casual greetings.

9. Commas between adjectives that belong together

Put a comma between two adjectives only if each describes the noun separately. The test: if you could put and between them, or swap their order, use a comma.

Right: a long, tiring journey (a long and tiring journey).

Right: a big red bus (“a big and red bus” sounds wrong, so no comma).

10. Inconsistent serial commas

The serial comma, also called the Oxford comma, is the comma before the final and or or in a list: “rice, fish, and vegetables.” American style guides such as the Chicago Manual of Style recommend it. The Associated Press and most British newspapers, including this one, generally leave it out. Both choices are correct. The mistake is switching between them in the same document.

Whichever style you follow, add the comma when a list would otherwise be ambiguous. Consider: “I would like to thank my parents, the prime minister and the governor.” Without a serial comma, a reader might think the writer’s parents hold high office. A serial comma, or a reordered list, removes the doubt. The Oakhurst drivers would agree.

When in doubt

Read the sentence aloud. A natural pause often signals a comma, though, as mistake No. 3 shows, not always. And if you still cannot decide, rewrite the sentence so that the question disappears. The clearest comma is sometimes the one you no longer need.
